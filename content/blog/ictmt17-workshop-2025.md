On Wednesday 22 October, the workshop day of the [17th International Conference on Technology in Mathematics Teaching](https://ictmt17.org.uk/workshop-program/) in London, Prof Manolis Mavrikis of the UCL Knowledge Lab and Prof Eirini Geraniou of the UCL Institute of Education ran a workshop on *Crafting AI-supported Exploratory Learning Activities for the Mathematics Classroom*. Vid and a few UCL PhD students helped run the session.

The room a mixed audience: trainee teachers, in-service mathematics teachers, and mathematics education academics.

![Workshop participants at desks with laptops in a school mathematics classroom, facing a screen showing the workshop title slide](/content/blog/images/ictmt17-2025-workshop.jpg "The workshop under way at St Paul's Girls School")

## Judging AI feedback

Participants first saw a demonstration of Snorkl - an AI tool that provides generated feedback on worked maths examples. 
Participants then took the user's seat, worked through a mathematics problem with Snorkl, and investigated the feedback provided by the tool.

![A pupil's handwritten working on a problem about three siblings paid different amounts for chores, beside an AI feedback panel marking the answer correct and querying one line of the working](/content/blog/images/ictmt17-2025-snorkl-feedback.png "The kind of AI-generated feedback participants were asked to judge")

The example above shows the sort of output that gets interesting quickly. The tool marks the answer correct — the pupil has worked out that all three siblings earn £4 an hour — and then queries a line of the working that does not say what the pupil meant it to say.

Participants rated Snorkl feedback responses on whether they were pedagogically appropriate in context, and on whether they encouraged mathematical thinking — a scale running from *too directive*, through *shallow questioning*, up to *strongly encourages thinking and reflection*. They could also add criteria of their own.

We also asked:

- If you were the teacher here, how would you respond to this pupil? Would you support a stronger and a weaker pupil differently?
- If you were happy for pupils to use a tool like this, how would you help them engage with its feedback critically rather than accept it?
- How might pupils use the tool to work out which feedback is actually useful to them — and to become more aware of their own problem-solving?

A round of agree/disagree statements on Mentimeter opened up where people stood on the teacher's role: whether it is part of the job to help pupils interpret AI feedback, whether such feedback scaffolds problem-solving or replaces it, and whether they would discourage its use during formative work altogether.

## Why feedback literacy

The thread running through the session was **feedback literacy** — the ability to seek out feedback, make sense of it, and act on it. It is a familiar idea in assessment research, and generative AI puts it under new pressure: feedback is now abundant, instant, confidently worded, and sometimes wrong.

This is a concrete example of agency in human-AI interaction applied to an educational use case. A tool that hands over a correct answer may improve the next homework result, without the pupil learning much. A tool that asks a good question at the right moment may do the opposite. Which one is more suitable depends on the task around it and the teacher shaping how AI is used.

Analysis of what participants said is ongoing, and a paper is in preparation. We will share it here when it is out.
