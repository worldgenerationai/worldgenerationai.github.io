On Tuesday 5 May, Vid, Isobel, and Jun presented a talk on "*Computer Science & Philosophy: be in Control when Using AI applications*" for [Beyond the Classroom](https://www.trinity.ox.ac.uk/trinity-enrichment-programme-2026), University of Oxford Trinity College's super-curricular programme.

Beyond the Classroom runs weekly and is open to any pupil in the UK who signs up. Sessions usually draw a few hundred students at a time, most of them in Year 12, with interests spread far wider than computer science — the week before ours was about nuclear fission. Each session is an academic taster, where we do a deep dive into a current research or academic skills topic, and then talk about careers and applying to university.

## Teaching a room you cannot see

The format is an online call with cameras, microphones and chat all off, which is sensible for safeguarding at that scale but does make for a quiet room. We built our session around live polls to engage the audience: rather than telling pupils what problems AI might cause, we asked them about their experiences, and incorporated their answers into the presentation. This informal evidence was the most valuable part for us as researchers.

## What they told us

We asked pupils to describe a time when they or their friends did not feel fully in control when using AI tools and how much it mattered. Then we grouped the responses into themes.

![A word cloud of the themes, with "bad outputs", "overreliance" and "other restrictions" largest](/content/blog/images/beyond-classroom-2026-wordcloud.png "Themes from the pupils' responses")

- **Bad outputs came first.** Participants were most often concerned about times when results from AI tools were not useful and they could not do much to make use of them.
- **Over-reliance was close behind**, and pupils were candid about it in themselves as well as in their friends.
- **Restrictions cut both ways.** Schools block tools, which pupils experience as making research harder rather than safer; and AI summaries in many search engines are unavoidable, pushing the sources pupils actually want further down the page.
- **Teachers using AI tools to mark** came up repeatedly, and it stung. Pupils found it especially unfair when their own use of AI tools is restricted.
- **Peer unfairness** was the other fairness complaint: classmates doing better by breaking the rules and using AI tools when they're not allowed, yet not getting any repercussions.

What we did not hear much about was **bias or persuasion**, which is where a lot of adult concern about AI sits. The things bothering these pupils were closer to hand: work that does not get better, rules that feel one-sided, and time wasted.

## What we took away

Pupils do not need convincing that AI has problems. They need something more useful than a ban or a cheerleader, and they notice when the adults around them apply different rules to themselves.

We took the opportunity to invite Beyond the Classroom participants to join us for our co-design studies by asking a parent to register via our expression of interest form.

Trinity College also hosted [our first Youth PRISM session](/blog/youth-prism-first/), where we worked with a much smaller group in person. Doing both in the same year has been a good reminder that scale and depth trade off against each other.
